\begin{frame}{Explanatory Variable \& Response}
\protect\hypertarget{explanatory-variable-response}{}
For each of the following claims, please identify the explanatory
variable and the response

\begin{enumerate}
\tightlist
\item
  ``\textit{Daily Exercise Improves Mental Performance}''

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Explanatory: Daily Exercise
  \item
    Response: Mental Health
  \end{itemize}
\item
  ``\textit{Want to lose weight? Watch more TV shows!}''

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Explanatory: Watching More TV
  \item
    Response: Weight Loss
  \end{itemize}
\item
  ``\textit{More ice creams sales have been linked to more deaths by drowning}''

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Ice Cream Sales
  \item
    Count of Death by Drowning
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Association and Causation}
\protect\hypertarget{association-and-causation}{}
Two variables are \textbf{associated} if values of one variable tend to
be related to values of the other variable. \bigskip

Two variables have a \textbf{causal (or ``cause-and-effect'') relation},
if changing the value of the explanatory variable influences the value
of the response variable
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Confounding Variable}
\protect\hypertarget{confounding-variable}{}
\textbf{Definition}: A third variable associated with both the
explanatory variable and the response is called a
\hl{confounding variable}

\begin{itemize}
\item
  A confounding variable can offer a plausible explanation for an
  association between the explanatory and the response variables
\item
  \red{Whenever confounding variables are (potentially) present,
  a causal relationship  cannot be established}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Experiment v.s. Observational Study}
\protect\hypertarget{experiment-v.s.-observational-study}{}
An \textbf{observational study} is a study in which the researcher does
not actively control the value of any variable, but simply observes the
value as they naturally exist.\bigskip

An \textbf{experiment} is a study in which the researcher actively
controls one or more of the explanatory variables
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Observational Studies}
\protect\hypertarget{observational-studies}{}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Many studies in social science and public health are observational
  studies.
\item
  Example: to study the effect of smoking on health

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    Treatment group (``exposed group''): smokers;
  \item
    Control group: no-smokers;
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Since the investigators have no control over allocation, the treatment
  group and control group might be different in important aspects other
  than the treatment, i.e.,\newline \textbf{there are many confounding
  variables}.
\end{itemize}

Observational studies only established \textbf{association}, NOT
\textbf{causation}!
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomization}
\protect\hypertarget{randomization}{}
How can we make sure to avoid confounding variables?

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.6}
\begin{tabular}{|c|}\hline
\LARGE\red{RANDOMLY} assign\\
\LARGE values of the\\
\LARGE explanatory variable\\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\pause

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  For experiments,the explanatory variable is ``treated or not''
\item
  Randomly \(\neq\) Haphazardly
\item
  Avoid the human factor: use coin tossing/random number generation.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Incentive for Smoke Quitting}
\protect\hypertarget{incentive-for-smoke-quitting}{}
Smokers at a big company were invited to participate in a smoking
cessation program and randomly assigned to one of two groups.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Reward Only}: subjects got a cash award if stopped smoking for
  6 months
\item
  \textbf{Deposit}: subjects had to deposit some money first, could get
  deposit back + a bonus if stopped smoking.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}r|cc|r}
\multicolumn{1}{c}{}&\multicolumn{2}{r}{\textbf{\large Reward Only Group}} \\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{}& Quit Smoking & Not Quit & Total\\\hline
  Agreed to Participate & 156 & 758 & 914\\
Declined to Participate &   3 & 100 & 103\\\hline
Total & 159 & 858 & 1017
\end{tabular}\\[5pt]
\begin{tabular}{@{}r|cc|r@{}}
\multicolumn{1}{c}{}&\multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{\large Deposit Group}}& \\
\multicolumn{1}{c}{}& Quit Smoking & Not Quit & Total\\\hline
  Agreed to Participate & 78 &  68 & 146\\
Declined to Participate & 30 & 877 & 907\\\hline
Total & 108 & 945 & 1053
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{}
\protect\hypertarget{section}{}
Which of the following is the correct and best conclusion of this
experiment? Explain your choice.

\begin{enumerate}
[(a)]
\tightlist
\item
  ``Reward'' is more effective than ``Deposit'' since the success rate
  of the entire Reward-Only Group \(159/1017\approx15.6\)\% is higher
  than that of the entire Deposit Group \(108/1053\approx10.3\)\% and
  the two groups are divided by randomization.
\item
  ``Deposit'' seems more effective than ``Deposit'' since the success
  rates among those to agree to participate in the Deposit Group
  \(78/146\approx 53.4\%\) is higher than that for the Reward Group,
  \(156/914\approx 17.1\%\). However, this comparison cannot establish
  causality since people self-selected whether to participate in the
  assigned program.
\item
  Same comparison as (b) but we CAN make a causal conclusion that
  ``Deposit'' is better since this is a randomized experiment
\item
  Same comparison as (a) but we CANNOT make a causal conclusion that
  ``Reward'' is better due to the huge difference between the
  participation rates of the two groups.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
